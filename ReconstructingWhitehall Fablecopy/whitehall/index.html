Whitehall Palace

Footprints start from the 1670 survey and are corrected, phase by phase, to Simon Thurley's plans of the palace, which rest on excavation and documents. Heights and details come from the pictures listed below. Click a building to read about it and to see what is evidence and what is estimate.

Buildings appear and disappear at the dates Thurley's phase plans give, or at a closer date where his text gives one (a building with a recorded span appears in the year it was finished), from York Place as Thomas Wolsey received it in 1514 to the fire of 1698. Buildings with no firm date are drawn faint until the earliest view that confirms them: Wyngaerde's drawing from the river of c. 1558 for the river front, the Agas map of c. 1561 elsewhere. Losses in the 1698 fire are shown by area. Labels change to the names used at the time.

    How to read this model
    • Where and when. Simon Thurley's phase plans of the palace (from excavation and documents) are treated as ground truth for where each building stood, how big it was and when it existed. Where a painting disagrees with them, the plan wins. Each photographed plan is straightened to its printed border and placed on the 1670 survey; all the plans share one placement, so they agree with each other, and with the 1670 survey to within about 1–3 m in the palace core. Buildings traced before this correction can sit up to about 2.5 m off the plan now shown.
    • What it looked like. Heights, roofs, windows and materials come from the pictures listed below. Where no picture shows a building, its appearance is an estimate; its card says so.
    • Faint buildings have no firm date. They are drawn faint until the earliest view that confirms them.
    • Galleries. Thurley marks many galleries "(above)": they were on the first floor, over rooms or over a lower gallery, and are drawn that way.
    • Ground cover. The surfaces on the ground are a separate layer. The model opens with them shown and the 1670 ground plan hidden; under Layers, raise “Plan on the ground” and lower “Ground cover” to see the plan. The street is shown as pebble paving, after Terasson's engraving of 1713; earlier views show it smooth or as bare earth, so that is uncertain before then. Garden walks are gravel, the park is grass, the Tiltyard is sand, and Pebble Court is a grass plot with walks until 1632, stone paving from 1632–3 and pebbles from 1660 (a visitor’s account of 1584, a paving record, and its name under Charles II); service yards and small courts are shown as packed earth where no view shows their surface.
    • The Thames is tidal. The Tide control raises and lowers the river over the foreshore and the stairs. Ground level and the foot of the river wall come from the sections of the 1939 excavations: the palace ground was about 2.7 m above Ordnance Datum and the foreshore at the foot of the wall about 0 m, so the wall stood about 2.7 m above it. The tide levels are estimates: high water is set 0.6 m below the ground, and low water 1 m below the foot of the wall, so that the foreshore dries out as Hollar drew it in 1643–4.
    • Names on the labels and cards follow the year: the Orchard becomes the Great Garden and then the Privy Garden, and the Low gallery becomes the Stone Gallery.
    • Thurley's plan for the current year can be laid on the ground under the Layers settings. Excavated walls are drawn solid on his plans; dashed lines are conjectural or upper floors.

    Historical Images

    The ground plan under this model was long known as “Fisher’s plan of 1680”. On Thurley's account it is the survey of the palace made for Charles II by Ralph Greatorex in 1670, engraved by George Vertue in 1747. The model calls it the 1670 survey; “survey no.” on a card is the number the survey gives that lodging in its key. Scale is about 0.255 m per plan pixel, measured from the scale bars on Thurley's plans (good to about 1.5%). It was first set at 0.23 from a Banqueting House 34 m long; Thurley gives 120 ft (36.6 m). The 1670 survey gives only outlines, so heights, roofs and minor lodgings are estimates; each building's card names its sources.
