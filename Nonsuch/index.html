Nonsuch Palace

Footprints come from Simon Thurley's conjectural plan of Nonsuch (fig. 85 in The Royal Palaces of Tudor England) and, where that plan is silent, from Martin Biddle's plan of the 1959 excavation. Materials and storeys follow the Parliamentary Survey of 1650; heights and details follow the views listed below. Every height is an estimate. Year

1568

Dates follow Martin Biddle's report on the excavation (1961), which works from the building accounts: begun 22 April 1538; Inner Court probably complete by 1544; Outer Court not entirely finished in 1547 and completed by the Earl of Arundel after 1556; Inner Court demolished by 1684 and Outer Court by about 1688. Buildings are drawn faint while unfinished, or until a dated view confirms them. Where sources disagree, the card says which was followed and why.

    How to read this model
    • Where. Thurley's plan (fig. 85) is treated as ground truth for where each building stood and how big it was. It draws the inner court in full and the outer court and the range between the courts in outline only. The north range, the outer gatehouse, the kitchen court and Cuddington church are not on it: those come from Biddle's Fig. 3, “plan as excavated 1959”, laid over Thurley's plan by matching the four sides of the inner court. The two plans agree there to about 0.4 m. Round the outer court Biddle's walls lie about 1 m east of Thurley's; Thurley wins, and what only Biddle draws is moved 1 m west to meet Thurley's ranges. Both plans can be switched on under Layers to compare them. The gardens are on neither plan.
    • When. Thurley's pages give no building dates. Dates follow Biddle (1961), with Historic England's scheduling entry and the Victoria County History of Surrey where he is silent. The model now runs to 1690 to take in the Stuart owners, the survey of 1650 and the demolition.
    • What it looked like. Materials and the number of storeys come from the Parliamentary Survey of April 1650 and from Biddle's excavation; the survey describes the house 47 years after Elizabeth I's death. Roofs, windows and the towers' shape follow Hoefnagel's drawing of 1568, the painted view of about 1610–20 and Speed's engraving of 1610. No source used gives a measured height, so every height is an estimate.
    • Faint buildings are either unfinished (the Inner Court to 1544, the Outer Court to 1556) or have no established date or position.
    • Directions on the cards follow Thurley's captions: the outer court is to the north, the King's side is to the west and the privy lodgings face south. These are conventional. Biddle's excavation plan draws north about 25° clockwise from the long axis, and Historic England describes the courts as aligned north-north-west to south-south-east, so the front the cards call “south” really faced south-south-east. The compass on screen follows Biddle. The north point on my photograph of Thurley's fig. 85 measured about 57°, which disagrees with both; I have set that reading aside.
    • Ground. The inner court stood eight steps above the outer court (survey of 1650; Biddle). Its surface is raised 1.3 m, an estimate at about 16 cm a step. Biddle says the palace was terraced back into a hillside rising to the south; outside the courts the only rising ground modelled is the hill of the Banqueting House to the west, 4 m high by estimate, with the winding steps up to it from the Grove of Diana. The rise of some 70 ft to the springs on the south, and the gentle fall to the north and east (both Biddle), are not modelled. The courts were paved: the outer and kitchen courts with flint cobbling and flagged paths (Biddle), the inner court with freestone (survey of 1650).
    • The plans can be laid on the ground under Layers: Thurley's as the photograph or as a red tracing, and Biddle's as a red tracing. On Biddle's, solid black is wall or foundation, hatching is conjectural or robber trench.

    Historical Images

    The ground plan under this model is Thurley's fig. 85, photographed from the book and turned so that the outer court is at the top. Scale is about 0.065 m per plan pixel, measured from the plan's own scale bars: the metre bar and the foot bar agree to within 0.5%. Two independent checks agree with it: the scale bar on Biddle's plan, and the survey of 1650, which gives the inner court as 137 by 116 ft against 139 by 117 ft measured on Thurley's plan. The photograph was not corrected for the camera's perspective, so positions are good to perhaps half a metre.
