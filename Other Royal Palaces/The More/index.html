A 3D Model, 1450–1700

The More

The lost manor of the More at Rickmansworth, Hertfordshire, from 1450 to 1700: the 15th-century brick house, Archbishop Neville’s alterations, Cardinal Wolsey’s enlargement of 1522–29, the royal house of 1530–47, its sale for materials and ruin under Elizabeth I, and the clearing of the site by 1661. Footprints follow Simon Thurley’s plans; dates and materials follow the excavations of 1952–55 and 2012 and a survey of 1568. No picture of the house is known in these sources, so every height and elevation is an estimate. Click a building to see what is evidence and what is estimate.

Year

1547

The model opens at 1547, the best-recorded year. 1450–1521: the brick house built under the licence of 1426; Archbishop Neville altered it in the 1460s, mostly below ground. 1522–29: Wolsey’s outer ranges, towers, long gallery and garden; the years are not recorded, so they are drawn faint from 1522 and solid from 1525, a chosen year. 1530–47: Henry VIII’s house, altered only in small ways; the labels take the room names of Thurley’s plan from 1530. 1548–1660: decline. The house was sold for its materials in 1574 and was “ruins” by 1598; Wolsey’s outer buildings are drawn roofless from 1576, while the gatehouse and the old ranges round the inner court keep their roofs. 1661–1700: every building is gone and only the moats remain.

    How to read this model
    • Where. Simon Thurley's Plan 10 (a conjectural first-floor plan of the house) is treated as ground truth for where each building stood and how big it was. On it, shaded rooms are the buildings that pre-dated Wolsey; the ranges in solid black are later. The photograph of the plan was corrected for camera tilt and laid flat as the ground plan.
    • Later evidence. The Wessex Archaeology report on the Time Team excavation of 2012 adds a survey of 1568 and a sale of the house for materials in 1574, neither of which the 1959 excavation report mentions. I know both only from that report. Where it differs from the 1959 report, the cards quote both.
    • When. Thurley's plan gives no dates beyond “pre-dated Wolsey”. Dates come from the report on the excavations of 1952–55 (Biddle, Barfield and Millard), which ties the brick house to the licence of 1426 and one set of alterations to Archbishop Neville, 1460–70, and gives the outer ranges to Wolsey because Henry VIII’s works are known from accounts and do not include them.
    • What it looked like. No view of the house is among the sources. Materials come from the excavation (rose-red brick in English bond over a chalk plinth, roof tiles). Heights, roof shapes, windows, chimneys and battlements are estimates; each card says so.
    • Windows and roofs. The excavated finds show leaded windows, some with painted glass of Henry VIII’s time and perhaps Wolsey’s motto, plain tiled roofs, and at least one Renaissance stone moulding and a carved chimneypiece. All came from the demolition layer, so they cannot be placed on any one range; they are quoted on the cards but do not change how any building is drawn.
    • Each card has up to four parts: a description, what comes from a named source, what is estimated, and what is known of the building after 1547.
    • Ruin. The sources say the house was decayed in 1556 and 1568, sold for its materials in 1574, and in ruins by 1598; the excavation dates demolition on the north side to about 1575 and the final clearance to 1655–61. The two accounts are not reconciled in anything I have read. The model shows Wolsey’s outer buildings without roofs from 1576, keeps the gatehouse and the old inner ranges roofed, and removes everything in 1661. A building “in ruins” is drawn without its roof and chimneys, with its walls at full height. That is a convention, not evidence: no source describes what the ruins looked like.
    • Faint buildings have no established date. Wolsey’s works are faint from 1522 to 1524 and solid from 1525 (a chosen year: the Treaty of the More was held in the house then, but no source says when the work was finished), and Henry VIII’s covered walk from 1530 to 1543, because only a span of years is known. The base court and the garden turn solid in 1534, when the accounts first attest them. The leats, the moated enclosures, the garden moat and the orchard turn solid only in 1547, the date of Thurley’s Plan 9.
    • Room names. Thurley’s plan names the King’s and Queen’s watching and presence chambers. Those are names from Henry VIII’s accounts of 1534–44; they are used on the labels from 1530, when Henry took possession of the house.
    • The phase plan can be laid over the ground under Layers. To 1529 it is the excavators' own plan (their Fig. 6), which shows which walls were actually found. For 1530–47 it is Thurley's Plan 9 of the whole site in 1547. After 1547 it is the excavators' plan again.
    • The wider site (river, leats, moated enclosures, orchard) is placed from Plan 9, which is coarser than Plan 10 and may be several metres out. These are laid flat on the ground, without banks.
    • North. Thurley's plans put north square to the walls, and the model follows them. The excavators' plan draws its north arrow about 12° east of the line of the walls. The two disagree and the sources here do not settle it.

    The ground plan under this model is Thurley's Plan 10, photographed from the book and corrected for camera tilt. Scale is 0.1 m per plan pixel. The plan's two printed scale bars disagree by about 3% (the feet bar gives 0.0645 m and the metre bar 0.0665 m per pixel of the corrected photograph); the model uses the mean, so distances are good to about 1.5%. The excavators' measured plan gives the old house a width of about 119 ft (36.4 m), which falls between the two. The plan is a conjectural first-floor plan, so ground-floor arrangements are not shown on it.
