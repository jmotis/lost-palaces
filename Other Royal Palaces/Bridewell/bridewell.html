Bridewell Palace

Footprints are traced from Simon Thurley's reconstructed plan of Bridewell (his fig. 54); the river Fleet, the Thames bank and the gallery to Blackfriars come from his conjectural plan of the waterfront c. 1540 (fig. 53). The 1978 excavation report (Gadd and Thompson 1979) and Stow’s Survey supply materials, dates and events. Heights and details come from the pictures listed below, and where there is no picture they are estimates. Black Friars, the Dominican friary across the Fleet that the gallery of 1522 joined to the palace, is traced from Nick Holder’s reconstructed plan. Click a building to read what is evidence and what is estimate.

Year

1540

Bridewell is far less well documented than Whitehall. There is one plan of the finished palace, not a plan for each phase, so the model has one building phase and one loss. The buildings are drawn faint from 1515, when the King took over the site, until c. 1523, and solid from then. What the Great Fire destroyed disappears after 1666; the three ranges of the great courtyard that were rebuilt in 1666–7 are drawn faint from 1667, because their new form is not modelled.

    How to read this model
    • Where. Thurley's reconstructed plan of Bridewell (fig. 54, “based on archaeological evidence and a detailed eighteenth-century survey”) is treated as ground truth for where each building stood and how big it was. Where a picture disagrees with it, the plan wins. On that plan, thick black walls are those excavated or surveyed; thin and dashed lines are reconstruction.
    • Scale. About 0.114 m per plan pixel, good only to about 4%. The page was photographed at an angle, and the plan's own two scale bars (feet and metres) come out 7% apart on the photograph; the scale used lies between them and was checked against the overall length of the palace on fig. 53.
    • When. There is one plan of the finished palace, so there is one building phase (1515 to c. 1523, Gadd and Thompson 1979) and one loss (the Great Fire of 1666). Events between are from Stow’s Survey of London (1603) and from Gadd and Thompson. Each card’s first line gives the years its building is shown, and says where a date is an assumption.
    • What it looked like. The only pictures from the palace’s own century are two small bird’s-eye views, the “Copperplate” map (surveyed 1553–59) and the “Agas” woodcut copied from it. The detailed views are of 1720, 1755, 1803 and 1806, after the palace had been a hospital and prison for two centuries and had been partly rebuilt after 1666. The excavation of 1978 gives the materials: red brick walls over 1 m thick, with Reigate stone dressings. Heights, roofs and windows are therefore largely estimates; each card says so.
    • Faint buildings are either under construction (1515–22: the order in which the ranges went up is not known) or, from 1667, ranges rebuilt after the Great Fire whose new form is not modelled.
    • The setting. The Fleet, the Thames bank and the gallery to Blackfriars are placed from fig. 53, which Thurley calls conjectural. It can be laid on the ground under Layers. It is coarser than fig. 54 and draws the long gallery and garden leaning to the east; the model follows fig. 54 there.
    • North. The compass follows Thurley’s fig. 53 and Gadd and Thompson’s plan, which both set the palace square to north. The north point printed on Thurley’s fig. 54 is turned about 25° from that; it is treated as the odd one out.
    • Where sources disagree. Gadd and Thompson (1979) call the south range of the great courtyard the Great Hall and put the chief rooms on the first floor; Thurley (1993) shows no hall, names the same range the King’s Presence and Watching Chambers, and puts them on the second floor. Thurley, the later work, is followed, and the cards say so.
    • Black Friars. The friary is traced from Holder’s reconstructed map of the precinct shortly before the Dissolution (2011, fig. 6, scale 1:1000), which rests on 38 archaeological investigations and on grants and surveys. It is joined to the Bridewell plan through the modern street plan: Bridewell is fixed by Gadd and Thompson’s plan of 1979, which is drawn over the streets of 1978, and Black Friars by Holder’s map of excavated sites on the Ordnance Survey; both were matched to today’s streets (Tudor Street, New Bridge Street, Carter Lane, Church Entry, Black Friars Lane). No rotation was applied. Footprints are good to about 0.3 m on Holder’s plan, and the position of the friary relative to the palace to about 5 m. On that tie the excavated east range of Bridewell and the guest hall of Black Friars are about 134 m apart. The gallery east of the Fleet then measures about 230 ft, matching the 240–250 ft of the documents; the bridge over the Fleet is extra. Its Bridewell end is placed where Gadd and Thompson mark the crossing, about 6 m south of the passage on Thurley’s plan, and it is still a little askew (see the gallery’s card). Almost every height in the friary is an estimate. The friary is shown as it was before 1538 for the whole period to 1666, with new names where Holder gives a later use; its end in 1666 is an assumption. The gates, the tenements marked “T” and the houses built after 1550 are not modelled.
    • Tide. The Thames and the mouth of the Fleet were tidal. The water levels in the model are estimates; no source used here gives them.
