Beaulieu

Henry VIII’s lost palace of Beaulieu (New Hall), near Chelmsford in Essex, from his rebuilding of 1516–21 to about 1700, a generation before most of it was pulled down. The north range, rebuilt by the Earl of Sussex about 1575, still stands. Footprints follow a plan of about 1700; its scale and compass bearing come from the excavators’ overlay of that plan on the modern survey; the look of the entrance front follows an engraving made before the demolition, and that of the north range the standing building. Most other heights are estimates. Click a building to see what is evidence and what is estimate.

Year

1547

Henry VIII’s buildings are drawn faint from 1516, when work began, and solid from 1521, when the recorded work ends. A lost earlier north range, known only from its basement, is drawn faint from 1516 to 1574; the Earl of Sussex’s north range replaces it in 1575 (Thurley’s “c.1575”). Buildings that nothing dates are drawn faint; those that the plan of 1624 also shows turn solid in 1624, and the rest stay faint. Nothing is recorded of changes to the buildings between 1575 and 1700, so the model stands still through those years, which is an assumption. The earlier house (the Earl of Ormond’s, licence to crenellate 1491, and its medieval predecessor) is not modelled.

    How to read this model
    • Where. A ground plan of the house drawn about 1700 (Simon Thurley’s fig. 63; the original is said to be at Boughton House, Northamptonshire) is treated as ground truth for where each building stood and how big it was. The photograph of it was straightened and laid flat as the ground plan. In 2009 excavation found the gatehouse, the south-east corner of the chapel and the west front of the house where this plan puts them, to within about 1.4 m.
    • How big. The plan as photographed has no scale. The model’s scale comes from the excavators’ drawing that lays the plan on the modern survey, fitted to the standing north range; a measured plan of that range made in 1921 agrees with it. Two other scales disagree and are not followed: the bars printed on the King’s Works redrawing of the plan, and the scale of feet on the engraving of the south front, both of which make the house about a tenth smaller.
    • When. Henry VIII’s campaign is dated 1516–21 from payments in the Chamber accounts, as reported by the excavators; no single building is dated. The north range is “circa 1575”. A plan of 1624 shows, roughly, what stood then. Everything else is undated.
    • Names. Great hall, great kitchen, great stair and tennis play are Thurley’s. Chapel, pastry, scullery, bakehouse, pantries, outer kitchen and stables are written on the King’s Works redrawing of the plan; the plan of 1624 confirms chapel, hall, kitchen and tennis court. The numbers in other labels are those written on the plan of about 1700, whose key is not reproduced.
    • What it looked like. The entrance (south) front follows George Vertue’s engraving, published in 1786 but showing the front before the demolition of 1737; heights on that front are measured from its scale of feet, and may be a tenth too low. The north range of about 1575 still stands and follows the Royal Commission’s description of 1921 and a modern photograph. No picture among the sources shows any other building, so elsewhere every height, roof and window is an estimate.
    • Each card has up to three parts: a description, what comes from a named source, and what is estimated here.
    • Faint buildings have no established date, or a form that is not known. See the note under the Year slider for the years at which they turn solid.
    • North. The compass shows grid north, which is 14° east of the top of the plan, from the excavators’ overlay. The model first assumed the top of the plan was north.
    • The phase plan under Layers lays the plan of about 1700 over the ground as red lines. It is the only plan used for positions, so it does not change with the year.
    • Not modelled. The house before 1516. The gallery or galleries of 1517 and 1521, and the conduit of 1521, whose places are unknown. The royal apartments: a description gives them a wing three storeys high, and foundations seen in 1968 north of the standing range suggest the palace once reached further north, perhaps round another court; the description’s eight courtyards cannot all be found on the plan. Damage from the fire of Henry VIII’s time. Buckingham’s works of the 1620s. Boundary walls, gardens, the long walk east of the house, and the paths and fountain in the great court (the plan of 1624 already shows them). A sundial of 1660 on the north range.

    Historical Images

    All three are reproduced in Thurley, and all are later than Henry VIII. The plan of 1624, the King’s Works redrawing of the plan and the excavators’ overlay are in the Oxford Archaeology report listed below; the Royal Commission’s plan and photographs of the north range are on British History Online. A bird’s-eye drawing captioned “circa 1580” on Wikipedia is a modern reconstruction with no stated source and is not used.

    The ground plan under this model is the plan of about 1700 in Thurley (fig. 63), photographed from the book and corrected for camera tilt. Scale is 0.0603 m per plan pixel east–west and 0.063 m north–south, measured from the 1:1250 overlay of the plan on the modern survey in the Oxford Archaeology report (their Fig. 2) and good to about 3%; the small difference between the two directions corrects the proportions of the photograph. On this scale the great court is about 45 by 55 m, the great hall 12 by 23 m, and the south front, from the west outer range to the end of the stables, about 160 m (530 ft); a description of Henry VIII’s time gives the entrance front as five hundred feet. The model was first built at 0.054 m per pixel, from the scale of feet on Vertue’s engraving, which made everything about a tenth smaller.
